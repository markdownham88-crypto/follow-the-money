§0Prologue: the little voice and the Monday thought

There is a little voice that keeps saying follow the money. It is the oldest instruction in investigation and the most neglected in a boom. When a whole economy agrees that something matters, it stops asking where the money goes once it has been spent. This essay starts from a conceptual sketch that lays John Boyd's final OODA diagram of 1995 over the AI investment cycle. The question at the foot of that sketch has become the question of 2026: are we observing genuine demand, or the consequences of investing in its expectation?

My approach rests on two instruments that are rarely used together. The first is axiology, the philosophy of value. It asks what kind of value is being priced, and for whom, before it asks how much. The second is the Gartner Hype Cycle, which maps how collective expectation runs ahead of real adoption and then corrects. The argument has four parts. The technological and competitive shifts are real. The valuations are complex. The Western Promethean impulse is technically ahead. But that impulse risks being underpriced, in both senses of the word, by Chinese technology that is cheaper, open and good enough.

Boyd explains how a reflexive cycle runs. Axiology and the Hype Cycle explain why it gets mispriced. The US–China contest shows where the mispricing will land. Jevons' paradox, worked through in detail in §6, explains the strangest feature of all: collapsing prices in one part of the loop can keep the rest of it spinning.

The money is real · Much of it travels in a circle · The question is where it leaks out

§1What the diagram is really showing

As drawn, the loop closes on itself. Capital funds hyperscalers. Hyperscalers buy from chipmakers and data-centre builders. Chipmakers' revenues confirm the forecasts, and the forecasts bring in more capital. Each pass through the loop produces "new information," but much of it is the cycle reporting on its own spending. In Boyd's terms this is a failure of Orientation. The observations are real, but the schema reading them treats the loop's internal transfers as outside demand.

Boyd put Orientation at the centre of his final sketch, and that was deliberate. In Destruction and Creation (1976) he argued that every mental model eventually drifts away from the reality it describes. The drift shows up as rising confusion. The only remedy is to break the model apart and rebuild it from fresh pieces. Applied to markets, a valuation is an artefact of Orientation: a compressed theory of the future, expressed as a number. When the inputs to that theory are increasingly produced by the theory itself, the model gets more confident while it drifts further from reality.

This is what George Soros called reflexivity. Participants' beliefs change the fundamentals those beliefs are supposed to describe. A rising chip order book is real. If the orders are placed because of the valuation, and the valuation rises because of the orders, then the order book is evidence about the loop rather than about end demand. The diagram's arrows labelled "implicit guidance and control" mark exactly where this short-circuit happens. Expectations shape action directly, without being checked against anything outside the loop.

The numbers show the gap. XTB, drawing on Morgan Stanley, estimates hyperscaler AI spending of about $760 billion in 2026, against AI product revenues of perhaps $80–150 billion.1 Other estimates of 2026 hyperscaler capex fall between about $600 and $700 billion. The ranges differ, but the ratio of spend to revenue is the same order of magnitude in every account.3,4 Futuriom's analysis finds that if the four largest hyperscalers fulfil their 2026 plans, revenues will grow by about 15.5% on average while capital spending grows by about 80%. Free cash flow is falling as a result. Oracle's capex rose from 37% of revenue in fiscal 2025 to 66% in the first half of fiscal 2026.4

The loop is also borrowing more. Morgan Stanley estimates that AI-linked debt issuance could reach about $570 billion in 2026, roughly double the year before. AI-related financing reportedly makes up about half of all US investment-grade corporate bond issuance this year.1 That is where the "follow the money" instinct should go. The money is real, but much of it travels in a circle, and a growing share of the circle is now funded with debt rather than equity.

The diagnostic question. For every dollar entering the top of the loop, how many cents leave it as revenue from end customers who would have paid without the loop's own spending, as opposed to revenue that is one participant's capex showing up as another's sales?

§2The Hype Cycle tracks expectations, not the technology

People often misread Gartner's curve as a judgement on whether a technology works. It actually plots the gap between two curves: the adoption S-curve, which is slow, real and cumulative, and the expectation curve, which overshoots and then corrects. "The shifts are real but the valuations are complex" describes the space between those two curves. The Technology Trigger, the Peak of Inflated Expectations, the Trough of Disillusionment, the Slope of Enlightenment and the Plateau of Productivity are stages in public belief, not in engineering.

Two points deepen this.

2.1 There are several hype cycles, one for each layer, and they are out of phase

Chips, hyperscale infrastructure, frontier model labs and applications each sit at a different point on the curve. The diagram's loop is how the chip and infrastructure layers stay at the peak while the model layer may already be heading into the trough. As long as hyperscalers keep ordering, chip revenue reflects the plans of the layers above it, not their results. A layer's position on the curve depends on whose expectations pay its bills. Chipmakers are paid from hyperscalers' expectations. Hyperscalers are paid partly from their own cloud franchises and partly from model labs' expectations. Model labs are paid from enterprise customers' results. Results arrive last, so the bottom of the stack is the first to face the trough.

As far as I know, Gartner's own 2025 Hype Cycle already placed generative AI past the peak and heading into the trough. Meanwhile capex forecasts kept rising. That divergence is the clearest sign that the layers have separated: the analyst consensus on the application layer had turned while the infrastructure layer was still being priced at the peak.

This is why Michael Burry's reported short on the iShares Semiconductor ETF matters as a signal regardless of whether it pays off. His argument is about a mismatch between layers: chip valuations, at a price-to-earnings ratio of about 74, have run far ahead of the big-tech customers that fund them, against roughly 32 for the Nasdaq-100.2 When the supplier is valued more richly than its customers, the market is pricing the customers' spending rather than their profits.

2.2 A trough is a transfer of value, not a destruction of it

The 2000 telecom bust is the obvious precedent. The fibre was real and became the backbone of the next twenty years, but the equity and debt holders who paid for it were largely wiped out. A technology can win while its financiers lose. Railways in the 1840s, electrification in the 1920s and fibre in the 1990s all followed this pattern. The infrastructure was overbuilt on borrowed belief, then bought out of bankruptcy for a fraction of its cost, and the cheap capacity became the foundation of the next era's profits. Those profits went to people who had not paid for the build.

That raises the question the Hype Cycle can't answer alone: value to whom, and of what kind? The curve tells us that expectations will correct. It doesn't tell us where the value goes when they do. That question is axiology.

The trough does not destroy value · It changes who owns it

§3Axiology: what kind of value is being priced?

Robert S. Hartman's formal axiology works well here. Hartman defined value as the degree to which a thing fulfils its concept. He distinguished three dimensions, ranked by how rich they are and how hard they are to replace:

  • Systemic value belongs to models, ideals and conceptual schemes: things that either meet a definition or don't. Here that is the AGI narrative, the "intelligence frontier," the benchmark score and the promise of the future platform.
  • Extrinsic value belongs to use and exchange: things that are better or worse examples of a class and can be compared and substituted. Here that is tokens, API calls, seats and workflow substitution.
  • Intrinsic value belongs to what is unique and irreplaceable, valued for itself and in its particular context. Here that means trust, institutional embedding, proprietary data, distribution, and regulatory or security standing.

Current Western valuations are mostly capitalised systemic value, a price on an eschatology, which is then converted into an assumed stream of extrinsic revenue. The danger is that extrinsic value is exactly what competition commoditises. Only intrinsic value resists substitution.

The categories are not interchangeable, and that is the axiological error in a bubble. Systemic value is binary: a model either meets the concept of "frontier-capable" or it doesn't. Once a cheaper model also meets the concept, the systemic premium does not shrink gradually. It disappears, because the thing it priced, being the only instance of the concept, no longer exists. Extrinsic value is comparative: it ranks the instances of a class and is set at the margin by the cheapest adequate one. Intrinsic value is singular: it attaches to this relationship, this dataset, this embedded workflow, and there is no market price that tells you what replacing it would cost.

So the real question about a frontier lab's valuation is not whether its model is better. It is what share of the valuation rests on intrinsic value that would survive if a model 90% as good became free. For many firms the honest answer is a small one.

3.1 The valuer's translation: worth versus market value

Red Book terms make the same point. Much hyperscaler capex reflects worth to a special purchaser, or investment value, rather than market value. Each firm pays a strategic premium because it can't afford to be absent. Market value assumes a willing buyer and a willing seller acting at arm's length, knowledgeably, prudently and without compulsion. Hyperscaler capex fails that test on one condition in particular: compulsion. One analyst described the dynamic as almost Darwinian. Any hyperscaler that pulls back risks being unable to buy capacity again quickly if AI demand proves real.3

Defensive worth set by fear of exclusion is a weak basis for the market value of the resulting assets. A data centre built by a special purchaser for its own strategic reasons is worth what that purchaser needs. If it is ever sold, it is worth what a buyer with no special need would pay. The gap between those two figures is the special-purchaser premium, and when the cycle turns it becomes an impairment. There are already early signs. Meta's move to sell excess compute reportedly sent the neocloud providers CoreWeave and Nebius sharply lower.2 When the most committed buyer starts selling, the market learns what the capacity is worth without the special purchaser.

Axiological thesis: the AI loop prices systemic value as though it were intrinsic, and books extrinsic revenue that open competition will reprice. The hype cycle's correction is the moment the market learns which dimension it was really paying for.

§4The Promethean inversion

This is where the China argument becomes more than a competitive footnote.

The Western AI project really is Promethean: capital-intensive, aimed at the frontier, driven by a sense of destiny, and actually ahead. Second Talent's summary of the evidence puts the average lag of Chinese models behind the US frontier at about seven months since 2023, citing Epoch AI. It reports that CAISI estimated about eight months for DeepSeek V4 Pro in May 2026, and that the gap on the Artificial Analysis index is about 16% of the top US score.6 The lead is real. The question is what it is worth.

The myth has a twist, though. Prometheus gave fire away. In Hesiod and Aeschylus, his offence was not inventing fire. It was handing to mortals something Zeus wanted kept on Olympus. Today the Western frontier labs mostly guard the fire behind closed weights and premium APIs, which makes them more like Zeus. The literal Promethean act of handing fire to everyone is being carried out by the Chinese open-weight labs.

The adoption figures show how far this has gone:

  • Second Talent reports that Chinese models carried about 61% of OpenRouter tokens by mid-2026, up from about 1% in late 2024. It also reports that four frontier-class open models shipped within seven weeks in spring 2026, and that Chinese models are 60–90% cheaper than closed frontier models for comparable work.5
  • The same source cites Vercel: in June 2026, open-weight models took 29% of its gateway tokens for just under 4% of spend. That is roughly a third of the work for about a twenty-fifth of the bill.5
  • The benchmarking firm Artificial Analysis has described a DeepSeek "death zone." To compete, a model now has to beat DeepSeek and its Chinese peers on price or clearly outperform them on capability.7
  • A RAND study, as reported, found that Chinese models run at roughly one-sixth to one-quarter of the cost of comparable US systems.9
  • One industry analysis reports that 19 of the 25 most-downloaded open-source AI systems on Hugging Face in mid-2026 came from Chinese organisations.8 Hugging Face's summer 2026 report counts more than 150,000 models on its Hub derived from Qwen, about 2.6 times the number derived from Meta's models.6
  • Amazon Web Services added DeepSeek V3.2, Kimi K2.5 and Qwen3-Coder-Next to Bedrock in February 2026, presenting them as frontier-class open models with much lower inference costs.10

So "underpriced" works in two senses. Chinese labs price under the West, and in doing so they reset what the market will pay for the Western lead. A frontier advantage of about seven months is worth a lot only if customers pay for the last 16% of capability. For most extrinsic, high-volume work they won't. Box's chief executive Aaron Levie has publicly warned that open-weight models like Qwen could trigger a broader collapse in AI pricing, since closed providers cannot justify a premium indefinitely as the gap narrows.11

4.1 The deeper difference is axiological, not technical

The axiological difference runs deeper than price. China is following an orientation it has used before: accept thin or negative returns at the commodity layer and capture value downstream, in industrial use, manufacturing, national capability and diffusion through the Global South. Solar PV and EVs are the precedents. The West invented the technology, China commoditised it, Western makers went bust, and deployment took off. The technology was real and the Western valuations weren't.

Chinese economic commentary has a word for the internal side of this: nèijuǎn (内卷), "involution." It describes ruinous price competition among domestic firms that drives margins towards zero. Seen from inside China it is a problem. Seen from outside, it exports deflation into every market those firms enter. The AI model layer now shows the same pattern. Several Chinese labs have cut token prices sharply through 2026, and some recent DeepSeek models are reported to be priced more than a hundred times below Anthropic's flagship Claude model.12 This is not irrational. If capability gaps keep narrowing anyway, the fight moves to who can serve "good enough" most widely, and the gains are counted in ecosystem share, industrial productivity and strategic independence, not in the model company's equity.

This is Boyd's "genetic heritage" and "cultural traditions" box in practice, where two civilisations orient differently to the same observations. The West sees a frontier model and asks what rent it can earn. China sees one and asks how widely it can spread and what can be built on it. Both look at the same benchmark tables and reach opposite conclusions about where value sits.

4.2 Tempo: operating inside the Western valuation loop

It is also tempo in Boyd's sense. Boyd held that the side which cycles through observation, orientation, decision and action faster, and can disrupt its opponent's orientation, wins. The aim is to get inside the opponent's loop, so that each of their decisions answers a situation that no longer exists.

The Chinese release cadence works inside the Western valuation loop. Each release is new information that the orientation pricing in the Western loop can't absorb. DeepSeek in January 2025 was the first demonstration. One model release, presented as having been trained at a fraction of Western costs, was followed by one of the largest single-day losses of market value in history at Nvidia. As far as I recall that was close to $600 billion. The technology did not change that day. Orientation did. Since then, releases have arrived at a pace that means Western price lists are often out of date before the quarter they were set for has ended.

Prometheus is ahead · Prometheus is guarding the fire · The fire is being given away

§5Where the mispricing lands: the loop redrawn

This is the part that makes the argument rigorous rather than just bearish. The risk isn't spread evenly around the diagram. Figure 4 redraws the original sketch, Boyd's OODA band above and the AI investment cycle below, and marks in seal-red the points where value leaks out of the loop rather than circulating within it.

5.1 The eight leaks, node by node

L1 · Phantom demand. This is the core reflexive leak. When a hyperscaler's capex becomes a chipmaker's revenue, which becomes a forecast, which justifies more capex, the system counts the same dollar several times as evidence. The more vendor financing, equity cross-holdings and compute-for-equity deals there are in the loop, the larger this leak. On paper it destroys nothing. It inflates the observation that every later decision depends on.

L2 · Model-layer margin. This is where the Promethean inversion hits hardest. Frontier model labs, the "AI Developers & Platforms" node, take the full axiological hit. Their valuations are the most systemic and their revenue is the most extrinsic and most exposed to substitution. When a third of the work can be done for a twenty-fifth of the bill, the price ceiling for any task that "good enough" can handle is set in Hangzhou and Beijing, not San Francisco.

L3 · Depreciation. Philipp Dubach calculates that the 2026 hyperscaler capex programme alone carries an annual depreciation charge of about $115–140 billion on five-year asset lives.13 Five years may be optimistic. Every gain in efficiency, including the ones Chinese labs publish in the open, shortens the economic life of the previous generation of chips. The accounting life and the economic life of a GPU are drifting apart.

L4 · Credit. As the loop moves from equity to debt, its leaks stop being optional. Equity can absorb a disappointing year. Debt requires coupons. Because AI-linked financing now makes up roughly half of US investment-grade issuance, the hype cycle is becoming tied to the credit cycle (§7).

L5 · Consumer surplus. This leak is good news for society and bad news for investors. If intelligence becomes cheap, most of its value goes to the people and firms who use it, just as cheap bandwidth after 2001 enriched the users of the internet rather than the people who laid the fibre. Value is created but not captured, so it never appears in the loop's revenue line.

L6 · Special-purchaser premium. Capex priced at strategic worth (§3.1) becomes an impairment when a non-special buyer has to set the price. The resale market for compute is where this leak shows up first.

L7 · Jevons migration. This is the strangest leak. It doesn't drain the loop. It redirects it. Cheaper tokens mean more tokens, which means more compute, more power, more grid connections and more land. Value moves towards whatever input is physically scarce. §6 explains in detail why this happens.

L8 · Orientation. This is the leak beneath all the others: a valuation schema that cannot distinguish the loop's echo from an outside signal. Boyd would say the loop is orienting on its own reflection.

5.2 Who is hedged and who is exposed

  • Hyperscalers are partly hedged. They host whatever weights customers want. Western enterprises worried about data governance commonly run open-weight Chinese models such as Qwen or DeepSeek on private US cloud infrastructure like AWS or Azure, so that no data leaves the company.8 Cheaper tokens may even raise compute demand through Jevons' paradox, which keeps the loop turning.
  • Chipmakers ride volume, with export-control and depreciation risk on top.
  • Frontier model labs, the "AI Developers & Platforms" node, take the full axiological hit. Their valuations are the most systemic and their revenue is the most extrinsic and most exposed to substitution.
  • Lenders sit in a separate position. Financing data centres against GPUs on five-year useful lives means taking security over assets whose replacement cost falls fastest exactly when the trough arrives.

§6Jevons' paradox in detail

Jevons' paradox explains how a loop whose prices are collapsing at one node can keep growing at the others. It is the strongest argument against a simple bubble thesis, and also the most widely misunderstood. It needs to be stated carefully.

6.1 The original observation (1865)

In The Coal Question (1865), the economist William Stanley Jevons dealt with a fear of his time: that Britain's industrial supremacy would end as its coal seams ran out. The obvious answer was efficiency. Better engines would get more work from each ton of coal and make the reserves last longer. Jevons argued the opposite. He wrote that it is "wholly a confusion of ideas" to suppose that the economical use of fuel means less consumption.

His evidence came from the history of the steam engine. Thomas Newcomen's atmospheric engine of 1712 was so wasteful that it paid only at the pithead, where coal was nearly free. James Watt's separate condenser after 1769 roughly quadrupled thermal efficiency. Later high-pressure compounding improved it further. Each improvement made steam power economic somewhere it had not been before: in cotton mills away from the coalfields, in ironworks, then in locomotives and ships. British coal consumption did not fall as engines improved. It rose enormously. Efficiency had not saved coal. It had unlocked demand for what coal could do.

The key move in Jevons' reasoning is to separate the resource (coal) from the service it provides (motive power, heat, work). People don't want coal. They want work done. Efficiency lowers the price of the service. Whether consumption of the resource then rises or falls depends entirely on how strongly demand for the service responds to its price.

6.2 The mechanism, formally

Let E be the amount of the resource consumed (coal, or for us, GPU-hours of compute). Let ε be efficiency, the units of service obtained per unit of resource (work per ton, or tokens or completed tasks per GPU-hour). The service delivered is then:

S = ε · E   ⇒   E = S / ε Service equals efficiency times resource. Holding output fixed, doubling efficiency halves the resource needed. This is the "engineering" or naive expectation.

But output is not fixed. If the resource costs pE per unit, the effective price of a unit of service is:

pS = pE / ε Each efficiency gain is a price cut on the service, even if the resource price doesn't move. A tenfold gain in tokens per GPU-hour is a 90% price cut on tokens.

Demand for the service responds to its price with a price elasticity η, defined as the percentage change in quantity demanded for a one per cent change in price (negative in the normal case, so we work with its magnitude |η|). Taking logarithms and differentiating with respect to efficiency, holding the resource price constant:

ln E = ln S − ln ε
∂ ln S / ∂ ln ε = |η|   (since a 1% efficiency gain is a 1% service price cut)
∂ ln E / ∂ ln ε = |η| − 1 This is the whole paradox in one line. The response of resource consumption to an efficiency gain equals the elasticity of demand for the service, minus one.

There are three cases:

  • |η| < 1 (inelastic demand): efficiency saves resource. Usage rises a little, but not enough to offset the gain. Some of the saving is "taken back," which economists call rebound, but consumption still falls. This is the case conservationists hope for.
  • |η| = 1 (unit elasticity): the gain is exactly offset. Resource consumption doesn't change and the efficiency gain becomes extra service.
  • |η| > 1 (elastic demand): backfire. Resource consumption rises because of the efficiency gain. This is Jevons' paradox in the strict sense.

6.3 The rebound taxonomy

Energy economists since the 1980s, notably Daniel Khazzoom and Leonard Brookes, whose work Harry Saunders formalised as the Khazzoom–Brookes postulate, have broken the paradox into channels. Each channel has an AI equivalent:

  • Direct rebound: cheaper service leads to more of the same service. AI: cheaper tokens mean longer contexts, more retries, more verbose reasoning and more calls per task.
  • Indirect rebound: money saved on the service is spent on other things that also use the resource. AI: savings from cheaper inference are reinvested in new AI features, fine-tuning and retrieval pipelines, all of which use compute.
  • Economy-wide or general-equilibrium rebound: cheaper service changes production structures, prices and growth, creating whole new industries that use the resource. AI: agentic systems, autonomous software engineering, AI-native firms and machine-to-machine workflows. These are uses that did not exist at the old price.
  • Transformational rebound: the service becomes infrastructure, and social practices reorganise around it, just as cities reorganised around cheap motive power and later electricity. AI: intelligence as a utility, always on, built into every interface, used without anyone noticing.

Backfire happens when these channels together exceed 100%. Historically that has been most likely for general-purpose technologies at an early stage, where the efficiency gain hits a service with large latent demand and many uses that no one has yet found. Coal in 1865, electricity in 1910, computing in 1970 and bandwidth in 2000 all qualified. The bull case for the AI loop is that intelligence qualifies too.

6.4 Why AI is a strong candidate for backfire

There are four reasons to think the elasticity of demand for machine intelligence is well above one, at least at today's prices:

  1. Latent demand is enormous and threshold-gated. Many tasks are worth automating only below a certain cost per attempt. Each price fall crosses a new threshold and opens a new tier of use cases. That is the definition of elastic demand.
  2. Agents multiply token use. An agentic workflow plans, calls tools, checks, reflects and retries. It can use one or two orders of magnitude more tokens than a single chat exchange for the same nominal task. Cheaper tokens make agents economic, and agents make token demand explode. The efficiency gain finances its own rebound.
  3. Reasoning trades compute for quality. Test-time compute, where a model "thinks longer" for a better answer, means each price cut can be spent on quality instead of saved. Users will rarely take the cheaper, worse answer when the better one costs only a little more.
  4. Open weights spread demand into self-hosting. Open models move inference onto private clouds and on-premises clusters, adding compute demand outside the frontier labs' own figures. That is exactly the AWS Bedrock and enterprise self-hosting pattern described above.

When the DeepSeek shock of January 2025 knocked chip stocks down, Microsoft's chief executive Satya Nadella responded publicly, as far as I recall, by invoking Jevons by name. His point was that cheaper, more efficient AI would make use explode and turn AI into a commodity people can't get enough of. The market's initial fear was a bet that |η| < 1: efficiency means fewer GPUs. The recovery was a bet that |η| > 1. Months of order books since then suggest the second bet was closer to the truth, at least so far.

6.5 Who captures a Jevons boom: the crucial distinction

This is where Jevons connects back to axiology and to the leaks in Figure 4. The paradox says the resource is consumed more. It says nothing good about the efficiency innovator.

In the nineteenth century, the great beneficiaries of more efficient steam engines were coal owners, railway companies and industrialists who used cheap motive power. Engine builders did well, but they competed in a market that soon commoditised, and Watt's patent premium ended in 1800. The value of the efficiency gain went to the scarce input and the downstream user, not to the people who produced the efficiency.

The Jevons–axiology synthesis: a Jevons boom raises the value of the scarce input and the downstream use, and lowers the value of the efficiency itself. In AI, Chinese open weights are the efficiency. They make the service cheaper and drive volume. The extra value goes to whoever controls the bottleneck input (compute, power, grid connection, cooling, land) and to the users (consumer surplus, L5). It does not go to whoever sells the model at a premium.

This is why the loop in Figure 4 can keep turning while its model node is repriced. Chinese releases are efficiency shocks. Through Jevons, efficiency shocks can increase demand for hyperscaler capacity and chips while decreasing what anyone will pay per unit of intelligence. The loop's volume and its margins move apart.

Revenue arithmetic at the model layer makes this precise. A model provider's revenue is price times quantity: R = pS · S. If prices fall because of competition, revenue rises only if |η| > 1 for that provider's own product. That is a much stricter condition than market-wide elasticity, because the volume can go to a cheaper competitor. Vercel's figures show this happening: open-weight models' share of tokens grew far faster than their share of spend. Market volume rose. The premium providers' share of the extra volume did not.

6.6 Where the paradox runs out

Jevons is not a guarantee. It operates under specific conditions, and every one of them can fail:

  • Elasticity is not constant. It falls as demand becomes saturated. Lighting is the classic example. Over centuries of efficiency gains it showed spectacular backfire, until rich households had all the light they could use, and then elasticity dropped. AI's first-tier uses (chat, drafting, coding help) may already be close to saturation for many users. The question is whether agentic uses refill the pipeline quickly enough.
  • Short-run and long-run elasticity differ. In the short run, demand is inelastic. New uses take years to invent, integrate and trust. In the long run it is elastic. But capex is committed in the short run and paid for over the life of the assets. This mismatch is the formal link between Jevons and the Hype Cycle: the trough of disillusionment is the period between short-run inelasticity and long-run elasticity. The build-out can be right about 2032 and still go bankrupt in 2028. Figure 6 illustrates this.
  • Efficiency on the compute side cuts both ways. Some efficiency gains (mixture-of-experts designs, sparsity, distillation, quantisation) reduce FLOPs per token instead of raising tokens per dollar of a fixed chip. If those gains outpace rebound at the hardware layer, GPU demand per unit of service falls even while service demand grows. Rebound then strengthens demand for some inputs (power, memory bandwidth) while weakening it for others (the current GPU generation), and that speeds up depreciation (L3).
  • Binding constraints redirect the rebound. If power or grid connections are the binding constraint, extra demand turns into higher prices for the scarce input rather than more chips installed. This is the strongest form of L7: the rents go to whoever owns the connection.
  • Export controls split the paradox geographically. In China, Jevons rebound from cheap open models hits a supply of advanced chips that is restricted by US export controls. The rebound therefore goes into domestic silicon, efficiency research and cheap power in the interior, which in turn yields more efficiency shocks for the West. Each side's Jevons dynamic feeds the other's.

6.7 Jevons as a hidden variable in the OODA loop

Returning to Boyd, Jevons is a variable most valuation models leave out of Orientation entirely. A model that says "cheaper Chinese AI means less Western compute demand" is orienting on |η| < 1. A model that says "cheaper AI means unlimited demand, so every node wins" is orienting on |η| > 1 and assuming the gains spread evenly. Both are wrong in the same way: they treat the loop as one undifferentiated thing. A better orientation holds three propositions together:

  1. Aggregate demand for intelligence is probably elastic, so the build-out is directionally justified.
  2. Value under elastic demand goes to scarce inputs and users, not to efficiency sellers, so the distribution of returns is not what the valuations assume.
  3. Elasticity rises over time while capex is paid for now, so the timing creates a trough that the financing structure may not survive.

Efficiency does not save the resource · It saves the user · It enriches the bottleneck

§7Hype meets credit: collateral, depreciation and the lender's view

Everything above matters to an equity investor. It matters more to a lender, because a lender's return is capped while its loss is not. The AI build-out is moving into the place where the Hype Cycle and the credit cycle meet. Morgan Stanley's estimates of AI-linked issuance, together with the reported share of investment-grade issuance, mean the loop's leaks increasingly turn into covenant tests.

From the point of view of a fixed-charge receiver, the questions are concrete:

  • What is the collateral actually worth on enforcement? A data centre has three layers of value. The land and grid connection holds value and has intrinsic, locational scarcity, which Jevons strengthens. The shell and mechanical and electrical plant holds moderate value but is exposed to specification obsolescence as rack densities and cooling methods change. The compute fit-out, the chips, loses value fastest, and every efficiency shock makes that faster. Security that relies on the third layer is security over a melting asset.
  • Who is the tenant, and on what terms? Neocloud providers and model labs are the most exposed nodes in Figure 4, and they are often the tenants. A long lease to a lab whose margin is being repriced by open weights carries the lab's axiological risk inside a property instrument.
  • Is the valuation at market value or at worth? A valuation that assumes a special purchaser's need (§3.1) overstates what will be recovered on enforcement. The Red Book basis matters.
  • Does the depreciation schedule match economic life? If the accounting life is five years and the economic life is three, earnings are overstated now and the collateral cover is overstated with them.

Seen this way, the most defensible lending is against the layer that Jevons makes more valuable: powered, connected land with flexible specifications. The least defensible is against the layer that open-weight competition and efficiency make less valuable: GPUs pledged as primary security, and tenancies whose income depends on premium model pricing.

§8The counter-case, stated honestly

The counter-case deserves stating honestly. Frontier capability may keep a premium where the last margin matters: agentic reliability, regulated and security-sensitive work, and sovereignty concerns about Chinese-origin models. If capability gains compound, a lead of about seven months could matter more than it does now. Open-weight adoption figures like OpenRouter's are also skewed towards developers and may overstate enterprise displacement.

1 · The last 16% may be the whole game

In agentic work, errors compound across steps. A model that is 95% reliable per step finishes a 20-step task about 36% of the time. One that is 99% reliable finishes about 82% of the time. Small benchmark gaps can become large gaps in completed tasks, and buyers pay for completed tasks.

2 · Trust is intrinsic value

For defence, finance, health and critical infrastructure, provenance, auditability and jurisdiction are part of the product. Western frontier models may hold intrinsic value in exactly the regulated sectors where AI spending is largest.

3 · Compounding leads

If AI begins to speed up AI research, a seven-month lead could widen rather than stay constant. The Promethean bet is a bet on compounding, and if it pays, today's systemic valuations will look cheap.

4 · Selection bias in the adoption data

OpenRouter and developer gateways favour cost-sensitive builders. Enterprise contracts, procurement inertia and bundling with existing cloud relationships may be far less elastic to price than those token shares suggest.

5 · Policy can close the market

Restrictions on Chinese-origin models in government, critical-infrastructure or regulated use could fence off a premium Western market and turn the commodity front into a protected one.

6 · Hyperscalers' FCF can flip

One view holds that the platform owners can compress free cash flow while they build and then switch to very large free cash flow once projects complete, re-rating their multiples. If that happens, the special-purchaser premium turns out to have been foresight.

None of these refutes the argument. Each one relocates it. Every counter-point is a claim that some part of the Western offer is intrinsic rather than extrinsic. The analytical task is therefore not to choose between bull and bear. It is to measure, firm by firm, how much of each valuation rests on value that would survive the free release of a model 90% as good.

§10Coda: Dào, Lǐ, Shì and the synthesis in one line

The Chinese triad 道 理 勢 (Dào, Lǐ, Shì) gives a closing structure for the argument.

道 Dào, the Way. The underlying movement is real. Machine intelligence is a general-purpose technology, and its adoption S-curve will continue whatever happens to valuations. The technological and competitive shifts are real.

理 Lǐ, the pattern. The principles by which value is assigned are where the error lies. The loop prices systemic value as intrinsic, counts its own echo as demand, and assumes the gains of a Jevons boom go to the producer of the efficiency. The valuations are complex because the pattern of value differs from the pattern of price.

勢 Shì, the propensity of the configuration. This is the strategic disposition that decides who wins without a decisive battle. In Sunzi's image, water finds the low ground and a rolling stone gains momentum from the slope. The Chinese open-weight strategy is a strategy of shì. It does not try to beat the Western frontier at its peak. It levels the ground under the peak so that value flows elsewhere: to users, to scarce inputs, and to a diffusion ecosystem whose centre is not in the West. A force that is more advanced but badly positioned can lose to one that is less advanced and well positioned. That is the precise sense in which the Western Promethean impulse is at risk of being underpriced.

The technological shift is real, and the West leads it. But Western valuations capitalise a frontier advantage as if it were intrinsic value, while Chinese open weights are steadily turning it into extrinsic, commodity value. The Hype Cycle's trough will fall hardest on whichever layer of the loop has priced the fire rather than the hearth.

One final turn. Jevons shows that the fire, once given away, will be used far more than anyone expected, and that its value will go to whoever owns the hearth, the fuel and the chimney rather than the person who gave it away. So the question at the foot of the diagram can be sharpened. It is not only whether we are seeing genuine demand or the consequences of investing in its expectation. It is also this: when the genuine demand arrives, as Jevons suggests it will, who will own the part of the loop it flows into?
